Kyoto Travel Guide: Top 10 Must-See Attractions

Kyoto served as Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years, and its legacy lives on in more than 1,600 Buddhist temples, 400 Shinto shrines, and countless gardens, palaces, and traditional neighborhoods. The city rewards both first-time visitors and repeat travelers, but its highlights are spread across a wide area, so planning is essential. This guide covers the ten attractions most worth your time, with practical details on tickets, hours, and how to visit each one well. Note that prices and hours can change seasonally—always confirm on official websites before you go.

  1. Fushimi Inari Taisha

    Kyoto's most iconic sight: thousands of vermilion torii gates winding up the forested slopes of Mount Inari. The full loop to the summit takes two to three hours, but most visitors turn back at the Yotsutsuji intersection (about 30–45 minutes up), which offers city views. The shrine is dedicated to Inari, the god of rice and prosperity, and is guarded by stone fox statues.

    • Admission: Free
    • Hours: Open 24 hours (shrine office roughly 9:00–16:30)
    • Tips: Visit before 8:00 or after 17:00 to avoid crowds; the gates are magical and quiet at night. Wear comfortable shoes for the uneven stone steps. It's a short train ride from Kyoto Station on the JR Nara Line to Inari Station.

  2. Kiyomizu-dera

    A UNESCO World Heritage temple founded in 778, famous for its vast wooden stage jutting out over a hillside, built without a single nail. The Otowa Waterfall below offers three streams said to grant longevity, success, and love. The approach through the preserved lanes of Higashiyama (Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka) is half the experience.

    • Admission: ¥500 (adults); ¥200 (children)
    • Hours: Typically 6:00–18:00; extended evening hours during special illumination seasons (check the official site)
    • Tips: Go at opening time (6:00) for near-empty views from the stage. Combine with a walk down through Higashiyama to Gion. Cherry blossom and autumn foliage seasons are spectacular but extremely crowded.

  3. Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)

    A Zen temple whose top two floors are covered in gold leaf, reflected in a mirror-like pond. Originally a shogun's retirement villa, it was rebuilt after a 1950 arson and is now one of Japan's most photographed buildings. The one-way walking route takes about 30–45 minutes.

    • Admission: ¥500 (adults); ¥300 (children)
    • Hours: 9:00–17:00 daily
    • Tips: Arrive at 9:00 sharp; tour groups flood in by mid-morning. Pair with nearby Ryoan-ji (rock garden) for a half-day in northwest Kyoto. The route is one-way, so don't rush past the main viewpoint.

  4. Arashiyama Bamboo Grove & Tenryu-ji

    The towering bamboo forest is a short walk from Arashiyama Station and pairs naturally with Tenryu-ji, a UNESCO-listed Zen temple with a celebrated landscape garden and views over the Oi River. The grove itself is free and open at all hours.

    • Admission: Bamboo Grove free; Tenryu-ji garden ¥500 (main hall additional ¥300)
    • Hours: Tenryu-ji 8:30–17:00 (last entry 16:50)
    • Tips: The grove is packed from 10:00–16:00; early morning is far better. Extend the day with the riverside Togetsukyo Bridge, monkey park, or a boat ride. The Sagano Romantic Train is a popular seasonal add-on.

  5. Ginkaku-ji (Silver Pavilion)

    Despite the name, this Zen temple was never covered in silver—its restrained, mossy beauty is the point. The garden, with its raked sand cone and pond, is considered a masterpiece of Japanese aesthetics, and the hillside walk offers views over the grounds.

    • Admission: ¥500 (adults); ¥300 (children)
    • Hours: 8:30–17:00 (March–November); 9:00–16:30 (December–February)
    • Tips: Combine with the Philosopher's Path, a canal-side walk lined with cherry trees that connects to Nanzen-ji. Spring and autumn are gorgeous but busy; weekdays are calmer.

  6. Nijo Castle

    A UNESCO World Heritage site built in 1603 as the residence of the Tokugawa shoguns. The Ninomaru Palace is famous for its "nightingale floors" that squeak underfoot as an intruder alarm, and for its ornate screen paintings. The castle grounds include gardens and a second palace.

    • Admission: ¥1,300 (adults, includes Ninomaru Palace); discounts for children and combined tickets
    • Hours: 8:45–17:00 (last entry 16:00); closed some Tuesdays in December–July and over New Year—check ahead
    • Tips: Photography is prohibited inside Ninomaru Palace. Allow two hours. It's centrally located and easy to reach by subway or bus from Kyoto Station.

  7. Sanjusangendo

    A hall housing 1,001 gilded statues of Kannon, the goddess of mercy, arranged in ranks—an unforgettable and slightly eerie sight. The 120-meter hall is Japan's longest wooden structure. It's a short walk from Kyoto National Museum.

    • Admission: ¥600 (adults); ¥300 (children)
    • Hours: 8:30–17:00 (April–mid-November); 9:00–16:00 (mid-November–March)
    • Tips: No photography inside. The statues are best appreciated slowly—look for the 28 guardian deities at the front. Combine with the National Museum or a walk to Kiyomizu-dera.

  8. Gion & Yasaka Shrine

    Gion is Kyoto's most famous geisha district, with wooden machiya townhouses and lantern-lit lanes. Yasaka Shrine, at the district's edge, is a major Shinto shrine and the spiritual heart of the area, hosting the Gion Matsuri festival in July.

    • Admission: Free (shrine grounds); Gion is a public neighborhood
    • Hours: Yasaka Shrine grounds open 24 hours; the district is best visited in the evening
    • Tips: Be respectful—do not photograph geiko or maiko without permission, and keep to public streets. Evening strolls along Hanamikoji and Shirakawa canal are atmospheric. The shrine is illuminated at night.

  9. Ryoan-ji

    Home to Japan's most famous dry landscape garden: 15 rocks arranged on raked white gravel. The composition is deliberately austere and invites quiet contemplation. The temple grounds also include a large pond garden.

    • Admission: ¥600 (adults); ¥500 (students); ¥300 (children)
    • Hours: 8:00–17:00 (March–November); 8:30–16:30 (December–February)
    • Tips: Sit on the veranda for at least 15 minutes to absorb the garden—it rewards patience. Combine with Kinkaku-ji, about a 20-minute walk or short bus ride away.

  10. Philosopher's Path & Nanzen-ji

    A tranquil canal-side walk named for a philosopher who strolled it daily, lined with cherry trees and small temples. It leads to Nanzen-ji, a major Zen temple complex with a striking brick aqueduct, a towering Sanmon gate, and sub-temples with mossy gardens.

    • Admission: Path free; Nanzen-ji grounds free; Hojo garden ¥600; Sanmon gate ¥600
    • Hours: Nanzen-ji 8:40–17:00 (December–February until 16:30)
    • Tips: Walk the path from Ginkaku-ji toward Nanzen-ji (about 30–45 minutes) in the morning. The aqueduct is a favorite photo spot. Nearby Eikan-do is spectacular in autumn.

Practical Tips for Visiting Kyoto

  • Get an IC card: A rechargeable IC card (ICOCA, Suica) works on buses, subways, and trains across the city—far more convenient than buying tickets each time.
  • Use buses wisely: Kyoto's bus network reaches most sights, but buses are slow and crowded at peak times. The subway and private rail lines (Keihan, Hankyu, Randen) are often faster.
  • Start early: Major temples are calmest at opening time. Plan your first stop for 8:00 or earlier and save crowded sites for late afternoon.
  • Check closure days: Some temples close on specific weekdays or during New Year; confirm hours before traveling to outlying sites.
