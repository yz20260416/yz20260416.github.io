Top Attractions in Toronto

Toronto, Canada's largest city, blends landmark architecture, multicultural neighbourhoods and waterfront green space into one of the world's most visitable cities. From the soaring CN Tower to the Victorian-industrial lanes of the Distillery District, the city rewards travellers who explore on foot. This guide highlights ten attractions that consistently rank highest for international visitors, each drawn from Toronto's established roster of sights and experiences.

  1. CN Tower — The 553.3-metre CN Tower, completed in 1976, is a communications and observation tower in downtown Toronto built on the former Railway Lands. It remains the city's defining landmark and a must-see for skyline views.
    Tip: Book observation-deck tickets online in advance and visit near sunset for the best combination of daylight and city lights.
  2. Casa Loma — This Gothic Revival castle-style mansion and garden in midtown Toronto was built from 1911 to 1914 for financier Sir Henry Pellatt and now operates as a historic house museum. Its unique architecture makes it one of the city's most distinctive landmarks.
    Tip: Allow at least two hours to tour the house and gardens; weekday mornings are quieter than weekends.
  3. Distillery District — This commercial and residential district on the lower east side of downtown contains cafés, restaurants and shops housed in the heritage buildings of the former Gooderham and Worts Distillery. Its 13 acres hold more than forty heritage buildings and ten streets, the largest collection of Victorian-era industrial architecture in North America.
    Tip: Visit on foot in the late afternoon, then stay for dinner; the pedestrian-only lanes are especially atmospheric in the evening.
  4. Kensington Market — A distinctive multicultural neighbourhood in downtown Toronto, Kensington Market is one of the city's best-known areas and was designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 2006. Its partly outdoor market is among the most photographed sites in the city.
    Tip: Go hungry and wander the side streets off Augusta Avenue; weekends are liveliest but most crowded.
  5. Ontario Legislative Building — This structure in central Toronto houses the Legislative Assembly of Ontario along with the viceregal suite of the Lieutenant Governor and offices for provincial parliament members. It sits within Queen's Park, on the former site of King's College.
    Tip: Combine a visit with a stroll through Queen's Park; check the legislature's schedule for free guided tours.
  6. Canada's Walk of Fame — This walk of fame on King Street and Simcoe Street acknowledges Canadians who have excelled in their fields, with maple leaf-like stars embedded in 13 designated blocks of sidewalk in front of Roy Thomson Hall and the Princess of Wales and Royal Alexandra theatres. The first inductees were honoured in 1998.
    Tip: Pair the walk with an evening in the nearby King Street theatre district, when the sidewalk stars are easy to spot under streetlights.
  7. Canadian National Exhibition (CNE) — Held at Exhibition Place from the third or fourth Friday of August through Labour Day, the CNE draws approximately 1.6 million visitors a year, making it Canada's largest annual community event. It began as the Toronto Industrial Exhibition and remains one of North America's top fairs.
    Tip: Visit on a weekday to avoid peak crowds, and check the official schedule for concert and air-show dates before booking.
  8. Ontario Place — This entertainment and park venue sits on three artificial landscaped islands in Lake Ontario, south of Exhibition Place and southwest of downtown. Opened in 1971, it has reopened as a park without an admission fee following its closure for redevelopment.
    Tip: Access the islands via the pedestrian bridge or by water taxi from the harbour; combine the visit with a walk along the waterfront trail.
  9. Leslie Street Spit (Outer Harbour East Headland) — This human-made headland extends about 5 kilometres from the city's east end into Lake Ontario, the result of five decades of lakefilling. It has evolved into a largely passive recreation area popular for walking and birdwatching.
    Tip: Bring a bike or comfortable shoes — the spit is car-free — and go early in the morning for the best wildlife sightings.
  10. Centreville Amusement Park — Located on Centre Island in the Toronto Islands, this children's amusement park has been operated by the Beasley family since 1967 and is open daily during the summer. The adjacent Far Enough Farm adds to its family-friendly appeal.
    Tip: Take the ferry from Jack Layton Ferry Terminal and plan a full day, combining the park with the islands' beaches and picnic areas.

Practical tips

  • Many of the city's biggest draws — the CNE, Centreville and the Toronto Islands — are seasonal, so check opening dates before planning a summer or early-autumn trip.
  • Book major attraction tickets (CN Tower, Casa Loma) online ahead of time to skip queues, especially on summer weekends.
  • Downtown sights such as the CN Tower, Canada's Walk of Fame and the Distillery District are walkable or a short transit ride apart; a day pass is often the best value.
  • Visit outdoor attractions like the Leslie Street Spit and Kensington Market in the morning to avoid afternoon crowds and heat.
  • Toronto's multicultural neighbourhoods reward wandering — set aside unstructured time between major sights to explore local food and street life.
