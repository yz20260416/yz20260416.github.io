Top Attractions in Milan

Milan blends Roman ruins, Romanesque basilicas, grand neoclassical spaces and world-class art into one of Italy's most dynamic cities. From the soaring arcade of the Galleria to the ancient columns beside San Lorenzo, the city rewards both first-time visitors and returning travelers. This guide highlights ten standout sights drawn from Milan's rich heritage, each with a practical tip to help you plan your visit around the city's neighborhoods and opening hours.

  1. Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II — Italy's oldest active shopping arcade and a major Milan landmark, housed within a four-story double arcade in the city center. Designed in 1861 and built by architect Giuseppe Mengoni between 1865 and 1877, it is named after Victor Emmanuel II, the first king of the Kingdom of Italy.
    Tip: Visit early in the morning for photos without crowds; it connects Piazza del Duomo with Teatro alla Scala.
  2. Basilica of Sant'Ambrogio — An ancient Romanesque-style Roman Catholic church located in the center of Milan, in the region of Lombardy. It is one of the city's most historically significant religious buildings.
    Tip: Go on a weekday morning for quieter contemplation; the surrounding area is easily reached on foot from the center.
  3. Basilica of San Lorenzo — One of the oldest churches in Milan, originally built in Roman times and rebuilt several times over the centuries. It sits within the city's ring of navigli, close to the medieval Porta Ticinese and near the Basilicas Park.
    Tip: Combine your visit with the adjacent Colonne di San Lorenzo and the Basilicas Park for a relaxed half-day.
  4. Colonne di San Lorenzo — A group of ancient Roman ruins located in front of the Basilica of San Lorenzo in central Milan. The columns are among the city's most recognizable Roman remains.
    Tip: Best visited in the late afternoon or evening, when the piazza comes alive with locals.
  5. Basilica of Sant'Eustorgio — A church in Milan situated within the Basilicas Park, long an important stop for pilgrims journeying to Rome or the Holy Land because it was said to contain the tomb of the Three Magi. It was probably founded in the 4th century and is named after Eustorgius I, the bishop of Milan.
    Tip: Pair it with San Lorenzo in a single walk through the Basilicas Park.
  6. Archi di Porta Nuova — One of the three medieval gates of Milan that still exist in the modern city, alongside the medieval Porta Ticinese and Pusterla di Sant'Ambrogio. Originally built in the twelfth century, it was restored in the nineteenth century with the addition of two lateral passages.
    Tip: It lies along the ancient Navigli Ring; combine it with a stroll through the nearby Brera district.
  7. Brera Academy — A state-run tertiary public academy of fine arts in Milan that shares its history and main building with the Pinacoteca di Brera, the city's main public museum for art. The surrounding Brera neighborhood is one of Milan's most atmospheric districts.
    Tip: Check the Pinacoteca's opening days in advance and allow extra time to wander Brera's streets.
  8. Arena Civica — A multi-purpose stadium opened on 18 August 1807 and one of the city's main examples of neoclassical architecture. Today it mainly hosts football and rugby games, concerts and cultural events, and can hold 10,000 to 30,000 spectators.
    Tip: It sits within Parco Sempione; check the events calendar before visiting if you want to see the arena without a crowd.
  9. Chiaravalle Abbey — A Cistercian monastic complex in the Chiaravalle district of Milan, founded on 22 January 1135 as a daughter house of Clairvaux. It is one of the first examples of Gothic architecture in Italy.
    Tip: It lies on the city's outskirts, so plan a half-day trip and check visiting hours for the monastic complex in advance.
  10. Basilica di San Calimero — A church in Milan named after Saint Calimerius, an early bishop of the city who died in 190 AD. It dates from the 5th century but was almost completely rebuilt in 1882 by architect Angelo Colla in an attempt to restore its original medieval structure.
    Tip: Look for the surviving 16th-century crypt with its frescoed vault; it is a quiet stop south of the Duomo.

Practical tips

  • Many basilicas close for a midday break; plan church visits for mornings or late afternoons.
  • Milan's center is compact and walkable — group the Duomo, Galleria and Brera into one day, and the San Lorenzo area into another.
  • Dress modestly for churches; shoulders and knees covered is the safest rule.
  • Outlying sights such as Chiaravalle Abbey require extra travel time — check opening hours before setting out.
  • Book major museum tickets (such as the Pinacoteca di Brera) online in advance to avoid queues.
